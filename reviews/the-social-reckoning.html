Movie Review - The Social Reckoning Written by Evan Turok David Fincher's 2010 film The Social Network is very close to being a masterpiece, making me care about an array of unlikable people and squeezing out every last drop of tension from such an intricate issue. I credit this to the meticulous director. His effect is sorely missed in the 2026 sequel ‘The Social Reckoning’, which is as dry and depressing as the very issues it tackles. Written and directed by the writer of the original, Aaron Sorkin, The Social Reckoning continues on after the true story of the creation of ‘Facebook’. This time we follow Frances Haugen (Mikey Madison), an ex-employee of Facebook who attempts to expose the media conglomerate's greedy willingness to promote hateful content as a means for financial gain. All the life has been drained from this series. What was once an unnerving, gripping thriller has become a melancholic math mission with no personal emotional weight. The fact that ‘private message shares carry a 3.2x higher algorithmic weight than a standard passive reaction’ means nothing to me and should not be the sort of statistics that become the sole focus of the film. (If it means anything to you, please call me and explain. talk slowly.) My point being, the detrimental difference between the two films is that the original understands that human connection is much more valued to an audience than a series of facts and statistics. None of the characters here are fleshed out or have any distinctive personality besides the mutual fast talking wittiness found in all Aaron Sorkin dialogue. But when every single character has the same personality trait, it ceases to be one at all. Visually, there was nothing defining about Sorkin's vision, a simple dot-to-dot approach that lacked a style and failed to do its part to create desperately needed tension to enhance this so-called ‘thriller’. Jeremy Strong plays Facebook creator Mark Zuckerberg with an uncanny perfection. His presence on screen is the clear highlight and elevates the film to a very enjoyable level, but is unfortunately not enough to save this ‘colour in the lines’, non offensive but emotionally void film.
